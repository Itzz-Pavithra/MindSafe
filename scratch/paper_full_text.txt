Repeated exposure to online harassment, threats, humiliation, and other harmful interactions can affect emotional well-being and mental health. Understanding this impact is important for identifying patterns between cyberbullying experiences, social media behaviour, and reported mental health impact. However, many existing approaches mainly focus on detecting cyberbullying or harmful content from online text and do not directly examine how individuals report their experiences and the level of mental health impact associated with them. This study presents MindSafe, an academic research platform developed to study the relationship between cyberbullying experiences, social media behaviour, and reported mental health impact. The system collects structured survey responses covering social media usage, cyberbullying exposure, witnessing experiences, frequency, coping behaviour, and reported mental health impact. The collected data is preprocessed to handle missing values, categorical responses, and potential target leakage before analysis. Statistical analysis is used to examine patterns and relationships within the responses, while a leakage-controlled Random Forest model is used to classify respondents into four levels of reported mental health impact: Not at all, Slightly, Moderately, and Severely. SHAP-based explanations are used to understand the factors contributing to individual model predictions. The proposed platform provides a structured and explainable approach for studying the relationship between cyberbullying experiences and reported mental health impact and provides a foundation for further research in this area.Keywords— Cyberbullying, Mental Health Impact, Machine Learning, Random Forest, Explainable AI, SHAP, Leakage-Controlled Classification, Survey-Based Research, MongoDB, SvelteKit, FastAPI.I. INTRODUCTION     Cyberbullying has become an important social concern with the rapid growth of social media and digital communication. People, particularly adolescents and young adults, may experience harassment, threats, humiliation, and other harmful online behaviour. These experiences can affect emotional well-being and reported mental health. Understanding the relationship between cyberbullying experiences, social media behaviour, and mental health impact is therefore important.Existing studies have often focused on detecting harmful content, offensive language, or abusive behaviour from online text. However, these approaches do not fully capture how individuals report their own cyberbullying experiences and their perceived mental health impact. Survey-based data can provide useful information about social media use, cyberbullying exposure, frequency, coping behaviour, and reported mental health impact. Analyzing these responses can help identify patterns and relationships within the collected data.To address this need, MindSafe was developed as an academic research platform for studying cyberbullying experiences and their relationship with reported mental health impact. It collects structured survey responses, performs data and statistical analysis, and uses machine learning to classify different levels of reported mental health impact. Explainability methods are also used to examine the factors contributing to individual predictions. The proposed approach provides a structured framework for studying cyberbullying and its relationship with reported mental health impact.II. LITERATURE SURVEY     A literature survey examines previous research, methods, and findings related to a specific research problem. In this study, the review focuses on cyberbullying detection, machine-learning techniques, explainable artificial intelligence, and the relationship between cyberbullying and mental health. Previous research has mainly focused on detecting cyberbullying from social media content, while recent studies have also explored explainable models and survey-based approaches. Reviewing these studies helps identify existing methods, their limitations, and the research gap addressed by MindSafe.     Alabdulwahab et al. compared classical machine-learning algorithms, including KNN, SVM, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, and Random Forest, with deep-learning models for detecting cyberbullying in social media data. Their results showed that machine-learning and deep-learning approaches can effectively identify cyberbullying patterns from online content [1].      Sultan et al. proposed a hybrid CNN-LSTM model for detecting cyberbullying from online textual content. CNN was used to learn important text features, while LSTM captured sequential information, allowing the combined model to improve cyberbullying detection [2].      Akhter et al. developed a hybrid machine-learning approach for Bengali cyberbullying detection using Facebook comments. The study combined TF-IDF feature extraction with Instance Hardness Threshold resampling to handle text representation and class imbalance, showing the usefulness of preprocessing and balancing techniques [3].      Balakrisnan and Kaity conducted a systematic review of cyberbullying detection research published between 2011 and 2022. Their review examined commonly used algorithms, features, datasets, and evaluation measures and highlighted the continued focus on machine-learning-based detection from online content [4].      Baiganova et al. proposed a hybrid convolutional-recurrent neural network for cyberbullying detection. Their approach combined different neural-network capabilities to improve the identification of cyberbullying patterns in textual data [5].     Santoro et al. proposed an explainable AI approach for bullying and cyberbullying research using survey-based behavioural information. Their work emphasized transparency and explainability when presenting AI-based results, showing the importance of making model outputs understandable to users [6].      Philipo et al. compared different text-classification methods for cyberbullying detection across social media platforms. The study examined transformer-based models such as BERT, RoBERTa, XLNet, DistilBERT, and GPT-2 and highlighted the importance of model performance, dataset quality, and computational requirements [7].      A 2025 study analyzed cyberbullying among 615 German university students using sociodemographic, psychological, and cyberbullying-related variables. The study shows that survey-based datasets can be used to examine cyberbullying in university populations rather than relying only on social media text [8].      Ajayi et al. investigated the joint detection of mental-health conditions and cyberbullying from Twitter and Reddit data. The study also incorporated SHAP-based explanations, demonstrating the growing use of interpretable machine learning for research involving cyberbullying and mental health [9].      Ajayi et a further developed a unified machine-learning framework for detecting multiple mental-health and cyberbullying categories from social media data. Their work combined classification with explainability and emphasized the need for clinically validated datasets in computational mental-health research [10].III. PROBLEM STATEMENT     Cyberbullying has become an important social concern as more people use social media and other digital communication platforms. Individuals may experience or witness different forms of online harassment, and the effects on their emotional well-being and mental health can vary widely. Understanding these effects is difficult, however, because cyberbullying experiences, social media usage patterns, and mental health impacts differ considerably from person to person. What is missing is a systematic way to collect and analyze information about cyberbullying experiences and their potential impact on mental health. Without structured analysis, it is hard to identify meaningful patterns, relationships, and differences among individuals. Simply collecting survey responses is also not enough on its own — it does not provide a way to categorize the level of mental health impact or examine how different factors relate to these outcomes. MindSafe addresses this gap by providing a structured platform for collecting survey data on social media usage, cyberbullying experiences, and mental health impact. The system analyzes the collected responses to identify significant patterns and relationships, presents the findings through statistical analysis and visualization, and classifies respondents into different levels of mental health impact. This gives a systematic basis for studying the relationship between cyberbullying and mental well-being, and supports further academic research in this area.IV. METHODOLOGY     In order to study the relationship between cyberbullying experiences and reported mental health impact, the methodology involves five main stages: Dataset Preparation, Data Preprocessing, Feature Engineering, Model Training and Evaluation, and Statistical and Explainable Analysis. Survey responses were collected through an online questionnaire and processed to prepare the data for analysis. The responses were then encoded into numerical features and used for statistical analysis and machine-learning classification.Fig. 1. MindSafe Architecture     Fig. 1 shows the complete MindSafe workflow, from survey response collection and data preprocessing to statistical analysis, mental health impact classification, SHAP-based explanation, and result storage.A. Dataset and Data Collection     The dataset was collected through a structured online survey covering social media usage, cyberbullying experiences, witnessing experiences, frequency, coping behaviour, and reported mental health impact. The survey produced 521 raw responses, of which 514 valid records were retained after cleaning and removing records with missing target labels. Two questions related directly to emotional symptoms and severity were excluded from the primary model to prevent target leakage. The remaining 13 survey questions were used as predictor variables.B. Data Preprocessing     The collected responses were cleaned and converted into a suitable format for analysis. Ordinal responses, such as age category, daily social media usage, and cyberbullying frequency, were converted into ordered numerical values. Nominal responses, such as gender and incident platform, were encoded using one-hot encoding. Multiple-choice responses were converted into binary features using multi-hot encoding. Missing responses were handled during preprocessing to maintain a consistent input structure.C. Feature Engineering     The 13 survey questions were transformed into 54 numerical features. These features consisted of ordinal or binary features, one-hot encoded categorical features, and multi-hot encoded responses. The resulting feature set was used as input for the machine-learning model.TABLE I. Target VariableThe prediction target is Mental_Health_Impact, derived from the survey question, “Do you think cyberbullying or online harassment affected your mental health?” The responses are encoded into four classes: Not at all (0), Slightly (1), Moderately (2), and Severely (3). The multiclass approach preserves the graded severity reported by the respondents instead of reducing the responses to a simple affected/not-affected category. Following the leakage-control approach described in Section III, the model predicts the survey-defined Mental Health Impact category and does not diagnose depression, anxiety, or any other mental-health condition.E. Model DevelopmentThe production classifier uses a scikit-learn Random Forest with 100 trees, the Gini criterion, no maximum tree depth, a minimum of 2 samples for splitting, a minimum of 1 sample per leaf, balanced class weights, and a random state of 42. Random Forest was selected because it handles the mixed ordinal, one-hot, and multi-hot features without requiring feature scaling. The balanced class weights help address the imbalance between the target classes without using synthetic oversampling. Using 100 trees also helps reduce variation compared with a single decision tree on the available dataset. In addition, Random Forest supports Tree SHAP for obtaining feature-level explanations.The dataset was divided into 80% training data (N = 411) and 20% test data (N = 103) using stratified sampling to maintain the target-class distribution. A benchmark model (Scenario A) was trained using the same data split and included the two leakage-prone questions, resulting in 61 features. A majority-class Dummy Classifier was also used as a baseline for comparison.F. Model Evaluation     The model was evaluated on the held-out test set (N = 103) using accuracy, macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score, along with weighted-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score. A confusion matrix was also generated to examine the predictions for each class. In addition, 5-fold stratified cross-validation was performed on the training set (N = 411), with shuffling and a random state of 42. This process was used to examine the variation in accuracy and F1-score across the five folds independently of the held-out test results. The results from both evaluation methods are presented in Section V.G. Explainable AI and SHAP     MindSafe uses SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to explain how individual features contribute to each model prediction. The TreeExplainer is applied to the Random Forest model to calculate the SHAP values for the predicted class of each respondent. For each respondent, the 54 feature contributions are ranked according to their absolute SHAP values, and the top five features are reported as the main factors influencing the prediction. Positive SHAP values indicate an increased contribution to the prediction, while negative values indicate a decreased contribution. The feature names are converted into clear, readable labels for interpretation. SHAP values describe the model's behaviour and do not indicate medical causation. In addition, the top 15 features are identified using their mean absolute SHAP values across the evaluation dataset.H. Statistical Analysis     MindSafe includes a dedicated statistical analysis module, statisticsService.js, implemented in JavaScript to examine patterns and relationships in the survey data. It provides four main types of analysis. Descriptive statistics summarize numerical variables using mean, median, mode, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum, while categorical variables are summarized using frequencies, percentages, and mode. The Chi-Square test of independence examines associations between categorical variables using contingency tables, degrees of freedom, the χ² statistic, and p-values. The independent two-sample t-test compares mean mental-health-impact scores between two groups, such as respondents who experienced cyberbullying and those who did not. Spearman rank correlation measures the strength and direction of monotonic relationships between ordinal variables.     Researchers can apply these analyses through the Bivariate Relationship Explorer and Statistical Analysis Suite. Each test is applied only when the selected variables meet the requirements of that test; for example, the Chi-Square test is used for categorical variables, while the t-test requires a two-group comparison.V. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONA. Dataset CharacteristicsThe analytical dataset contains 514 respondents, distributed across four Mental Health Impact classes, as shown in Fig. 2. The distribution is strongly skewed toward the Not at all class, with 44.55% of respondents reporting no perceived mental-health impact, compared with only 7.98% reporting a Severe impact. This class imbalance presents a challenge for the classification task described in Section IV-E.Fig. 2. Distribution of respondents across Mental Health Impact classes (N = 514).B. Statistical Analysis ResultsThe statistical module supports descriptive statistics, Chi-Square testing, Spearman correlation, and independent two-sample t-testing, as described in Section IV-H. Descriptive analysis showed a mean Emotional Impact Severity of 2.100 (median = 2.0, mode = 1.0, SD = 1.208) on the 1–5 Likert scale, a mean Cyberbullying Encounter Frequency of 0.951 (SD = 1.001) on the 0–4 ordinal scale, a mean daily social-media usage of 3.784 hours (SD = 2.707), and a mean Mental Health Impact score of 0.922 (SD = 0.984) on the 0–3 target scale. Chi-Square analysis showed significant associations between Mental Health Impact and direct cyberbullying experience (χ² = 146.281, df = 3, p = 1.67 × 10⁻³¹, Cramér's V = 0.534), encounter frequency (χ² = 51.744, df = 12, p = 6.88 × 10⁻⁷, V = 0.184), and incident platform (χ² = 21.736, df = 12, p = 0.0406, V = 0.119). Gender identity (χ² = 13.588, df = 9, p = 0.1377, V = 0.094) and daily usage hours (χ² = 8.981, df = 9, p = 0.4390, V = 0.076) did not show statistically significant associations. Spearman correlation showed a moderate positive relationship between Emotional Impact Severity and Mental Health Impact (ρ = 0.319, p = 1.85 × 10⁻¹³). Weaker positive relationships were observed between Cyberbullying Encounter Frequency and Mental Health Impact (ρ = 0.242, p = 3.02 × 10⁻⁸) and between Cyberbullying Encounter Frequency and Emotional Impact Severity (ρ = 0.278, p = 2.08 × 10⁻¹⁰). Daily social-media usage showed a negligible and non-significant relationship with Mental Health Impact (ρ = 0.029, p = 0.7335).C. Model Comparison/Fig. 3. CONCLUSION     This paper presented MindSafe, an academic research platform that predicts a survey-defined Mental Health Impact category from cyberbullying-related survey responses. The platform uses a leakage-controlled Random Forest classifier and Tree SHAP to explain individual predictions. On a held-out test set of 103 respondents, the model achieved 48.54% accuracy and a macro F1-score of 0.3275. Its cross-validated accuracy of 49.88% ± 1.74% provides additional evidence of consistent performance across different training folds. MindSafe combines this prediction workflow with a three-tier web application using a SvelteKit frontend, FastAPI machine-learning service, and MongoDB Atlas, along with statistical tools for exploring the survey data.     The results also show the limitations of the current model. The Severely class had a recall of only 12.50%, reflecting the difficulty of predicting minority classes in the relatively small and imbalanced survey dataset (N = 514). Therefore, MindSafe is presented as a non-diagnostic research and screening-awareness platform, not as a clinical diagnostic system. It predicts a survey-defined mental health impact category rather than diagnosing a mental health condition. Future work can use larger and more diverse datasets to further evaluate and improve the approach.VII. FUTURE ENHANCEMENTS     Several areas can be improved in future work. First, the dataset should be expanded beyond 514 respondents and collected from more than one institution. This could improve the results, especially for the Severely class, which currently has a low recall of 12.50%. Second, the difference between the production and training software versions should be resolved: the production environment uses pandas 1.5.3 and SHAP 0.42.1, while training used pandas 2.2.3 and SHAP 0.49.1. Retraining and testing the model in the same environment would reduce possible compatibility problems. Third, the security limitations should be improved, since the current system uses a fallback JWT secret and has no rate limiting for the login, registration, and prediction endpoints. Fourth, collecting data from the same participants over multiple time periods could help study changes and patterns over time, although this would still not prove a clinical cause-and-effect relationship. Finally, future work could compare MindSafe's results with validated clinical mental-health questionnaires, with proper ethical approval. Until then, MindSafe should remain focused on research and educational awareness, rather than clinical use.REFERENCES[1] H. Alabdulwahab, M. A. Haq, and M.
