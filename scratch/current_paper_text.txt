Repeated exposure to harassment, threats, humiliation, and other harmful interactions can affect emotional well-being and mental health. Understanding this impact is important for identifying patterns between cyberbullying experiences, social media behaviour, and reported mental health impact. However, many existing approaches mainly focus on detecting cyberbullying or harmful content from text and do not directly examine how individuals report their experiences and the level of mental health impact associated with them. This study presents MindSafe, a research platform developed to study the relationship between cyberbullying experiences, social media behaviour, and reported mental health impact. The system collects survey responses covering social media usage, cyberbullying exposure, witnessing experiences, frequency, coping behaviour, and reported mental health impact. The collected data is pre processed to handle missing values, categorical responses, and potential target leakage before analysis. Statistical analysis is used to examine patterns and relationships within the responses. Based on the survey responses, an individual's  mental health impact is classified using the Random Forest method. The contributing factors associated with variations in reported mental health impact are identified using SHAP.      Keywords — Cyberbullying, Mental Health Impact, Machine Learning, Random Forest, Explainable AI, SHAP, Leakage-Controlled Classification, Survey-Based Research, MongoDB, SvelteKit, FastAPI.I. INTRODUCTIONCyberbullying has become an increasingly widespread social problem rise of social media and other digital communication channels. While enabling communication and interaction, such channels also facilitate insults, threats, humiliation, harassment, and other forms of cyberbullying that may adversely affect the well-being of the victims. Such effects may be different for different individuals, circumstances, and exposure levels. Victims of cyberbullying can experience a wide range of emotions from anger and sadness to anxiety and depression. Cyberbullying can take many forms and can be experienced or observed through various digital communication channels. The frequency and impact can vary from one individual to another, depending on the circumstances and the response to the incident. Some individuals may report or seek help in responding to cyberbullying while others do not. This information is useful in understanding the experiences of cyberbullying and their impact on individuals.Research to date has focused on detecting incidents of cyberbullying, offensive language or messages, or other undesirable topics or actions in the digital space. This approach is vital in identifying undesirable activities and detecting them early. However, it does not focus on exploring how individuals experience and explain the situation or relate it to the impact on their well-being. Alternatively, a survey-based approach is necessary to complement the identified research gap. This approach would rely on information provided by the participants and explore their experiences, behaviors, and beliefs regarding cyberbullying and relate them to the reported impact.The survey method can be utilized by asking participants to self-report on their experiences with cyberbullying, including identifying if they have been victims or witnesses. Additionally, the survey can be structured to collect information on how often such experiences occur, where they occur, and what actions have been taken in response to such experiences. This information allows us to explore the variation in survey responses, which can indicate a range of factors associated with differences in experiences and behaviors.With the survey results, it will be possible to identify associations between experiences and impact and develop a set of criteria that can be used to predict the likelihood of a respondent reporting a negative impact on mental well-being. Statistical analysis will be necessary to explore possible associations between the variables. Additionally, a machine learning algorithm can be utilized to classify the survey respondents according to their mental well-being impact reports based on the survey variables. An explainer will also be necessary to demonstrate how the variables contribute to each respondent’s outcome rather than being presented as an automated black-box model.It will be possible to design and implement the described research platform, which will be referred to as MindSafe . This platform will rely on survey research, statistical analysis, and machine learning to explore the characteristics of cyberbullying experiences and impact on mental well-being. The tool will be designed for research purposes and will be utilized to analyze the survey data to identify patterns that can assist in understanding the relationship between experiences and impact.II. LITERATURE SURVEY     A literature survey examines previous research, methods, and findings related to a specific research problem. In this study, the review focuses on cyberbullying detection, machine-learning techniques, explainable artificial intelligence, and the relationship between cyberbullying and mental health. Previous research has mainly focused on detecting cyberbullying from social media content, while recent studies have also explored explainable models and survey-based approaches. Reviewing these studies helps identify existing methods, their limitations, and the research gap addressed by MindSafe.     Alabdulwahab et al. compared classical machine-learning algorithms, including KNN, SVM, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree, and Random Forest, with deep-learning models for detecting cyberbullying in social media data. Their results showed that machine-learning and deep-learning approaches can effectively identify cyberbullying patterns from online content [1].      Sultan et al. proposed a hybrid CNN-LSTM model for detecting cyberbullying from online textual content. CNN was used to learn important text features, while LSTM captured sequential information, allowing the combined model to improve cyberbullying detection [2].      Akhter et al. developed a hybrid machine-learning approach for Bengali cyberbullying detection using Facebook comments. The study combined TF-IDF feature extraction with Instance Hardness Threshold resampling to handle text representation and class imbalance, showing the usefulness of preprocessing and balancing techniques [3].      Balakrisnan and Kaity conducted a systematic review of cyberbullying detection research published between 2011 and 2022. Their review examined commonly used algorithms, features, datasets, and evaluation measures and highlighted the continued focus on machine-learning-based detection from online content [4].      Baiganova et al. proposed a hybrid convolutional-recurrent neural network for cyberbullying detection. Their approach combined different neural-network capabilities to improve the identification of cyberbullying patterns in textual data [5].     Santoro et al. proposed an explainable AI approach for bullying and cyberbullying research using survey-based behavioural information. Their work emphasized transparency and explainability when presenting AI-based results, showing the importance of making model outputs understandable to users [6].      Philipo et al. compared different text-classification methods for cyberbullying detection across social media platforms. The study examined transformer-based models such as BERT, RoBERTa, XLNet, DistilBERT, and GPT-2 and highlighted the importance of model performance, dataset quality, and computational requirements [7].      Ranjith et al. studied cyberbullying among 484 school-going adolescents in India using questionnaire-based measures of cyberbullying, mental health, coping behaviour, and internet usage. The study used statistical modelling to examine factors associated with cyber-victimization and cyber-offending, showing the value of structured survey data for studying cyberbullying experiences and related psychosocial factors [8].     Arif et al. conducted a systematic review of studies examining the impact of cyberbullying on mental health among university students. The review found that cyberbullying was associated with several adverse mental-health outcomes, including depression, anxiety, stress, and suicidal behaviour, highlighting the importance of studying cyberbullying experiences together with mental-health outcomes [9].      Ajayi et al. proposed a machine-learning framework for detecting mental-health conditions and cyberbullying from Twitter and Reddit data. Their study combined classification with SHAP-based explanations and highlighted the need for clinically validated datasets in computational mental-health research [10].      The reviewed studies mainly focus on detecting cyberbullying using social media content and text-based data. Some recent studies have also explored survey-based analysis, mental-health impact, and explainable AI. However, fewer studies combine these approaches in a single platform. MindSafe addresses this gap by using structured survey data to study cyberbullying experiences and reported mental health impact. It combines statistical analysis, a leakage-controlled Random Forest model for prediction, and SHAP for explaining the factors behind the predictions.III. PROBLEM STATEMENT     Cyberbullying has become an important social concern as more people use social media and other digital communication platforms. Individuals may experience or witness different forms of online harassment, and the effects on their emotional well-being and mental health can vary widely. Understanding these effects is difficult, however, because cyberbullying experiences, social media usage patterns, and mental health impacts differ considerably from person to person. What is missing is a systematic way to collect and analyze information about cyberbullying experiences and their potential impact on mental health. Without structured analysis, it is hard to identify meaningful patterns, relationships, and differences among individuals. Simply collecting survey responses is also not enough on its own  it does not provide a way to categorize the level of mental health impact or examine how different factors relate to these outcomes. MindSafe addresses this gap by providing a structured platform for collecting survey data on social media usage, cyberbullying experiences, and mental health impact. The system analyzes the collected responses to identify significant patterns and relationships, presents the findings through statistical analysis and visualization, and classifies respondents into different levels of mental health impact. This gives a systematic basis for studying the relationship between cyberbullying and mental well-being, and supports further academic research in this area.IV. METHODOLOGY     In order to study the relationship between cyberbullying experiences and reported mental health impact, the methodology involves five main stages: Dataset Preparation, Data Preprocessing, Feature Engineering, Model Training and Evaluation, and Statistical and Explainable Analysis. Survey responses were collected through an online questionnaire and processed to prepare the data for analysis. The responses were then encoded into numerical features and used for statistical analysis and machine-learning classification./Fig. 1. MindSafe Architecture     Fig. 1 shows the complete MindSafe workflow, from survey response collection and data preprocessing to statistical analysis, mental health impact classification, SHAP-based explanation, and result storage.A. Dataset and Data Collection     The dataset was collected through a structured online survey covering social media usage, cyberbullying experiences, witnessing experiences, frequency, coping behaviour, and reported mental health impact. The survey produced 521 raw responses, of which 514 valid records were retained after cleaning and removing records with missing target labels. Two questions related directly to emotional symptoms and severity were excluded from the primary model to prevent target leakage. The remaining 13 survey questions were used as predictor variables.B. Data Preprocessing     The collected responses were cleaned and converted into a suitable format for analysis. Ordinal responses, such as age category, daily social media usage, and cyberbullying frequency, were converted into ordered numerical values. Nominal responses, such as gender and incident platform, were encoded using one-hot encoding. Multiple-choice responses were converted into binary features using multi-hot encoding. Missing responses were handled during preprocessing to maintain a consistent input structure.C. Feature Engineering     The 13 survey questions were transformed into 54 numerical features. These features consisted of ordinal or binary features, one-hot encoded categorical features, and multi-hot encoded responses. The resulting feature set was used as input for the machine-learning model.TABLE I. Target VariableThe prediction target is Mental_Health_Impact, derived from the survey question, “To what extent has cyberbullying affected your mental health?” The responses are encoded into four classes: Not at all (0), Slightly (1), Moderately (2), and Severely (3). The multiclass approach preserves the graded severity reported by the respondents instead of reducing the responses to a simple affected/not-affected category. Following the leakage-control approach described in Section III, the model predicts the survey-defined Mental Health Impact category and does not diagnose depression, anxiety, or any other mental-health condition.E. Model DevelopmentMindSafe uses a Random Forest classifier to predict the reported mental health impact from the survey responses. The model is developed using the cleaned survey dataset. Before training, the survey data is checked for missing values, and the responses are converted into a suitable numerical format. Features that could cause target leakage are excluded so that the model does not use information that directly reveals the outcome.The system uses 13 selected survey items, which are converted into 54 numerical features through different encoding methods. These features represent information such as age, social-media usage, cyberbullying experience, witnessed cyberbullying, frequency of incidents, platforms involved, types of cyberbullying, help-seeking behaviour, and actions taken by respondents.The processed features are then given to the Random Forest model for training. Random Forest works by building multiple decision trees and combining their results to make a final prediction. In MindSafe, the model uses the available survey features to learn patterns between cyberbullying-related experiences and the reported mental health impact. The model also produces probabilities for the possible outcome categories.To reduce the effect of unequal numbers of respondents across the outcome categories, the model uses balanced class weights during training. This helps the classifier give appropriate consideration to classes that have fewer observations instead of being strongly influenced by the majority class.After training, the model is tested using a separate test dataset that was not used during model training. This helps measure how the model performs on unseen responses. The model is evaluated using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and a confusion matrix. Five-fold cross-validation is also used to check the consistency of the model across different portions of the dataset.MindSafe also uses SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to explain the model's predictions. SHAP identifies the features that contributed most to an individual prediction. It shows whether a feature supports the predicted outcome or moves the prediction away from it. For example, if a respondent's cyberbullying experience has a positive contribution toward the predicted outcome, SHAP shows that this feature supported that prediction. This makes the model's output easier to understand rather than showing only the final prediction.The final result combines the model prediction, class probabilities, and SHAP-based explanation. This allows MindSafe to provide not only the predicted reported mental health impact but also information about the survey responses that contributed to the model's decision.F. Model Evaluation     The model was evaluated on the held-out test set (N = 103) using accuracy, macro-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score, along with weighted-averaged precision, recall, and F1-score. A confusion matrix was also generated to examine the predictions for each class. In addition, 5-fold stratified cross-validation was performed on the training set (N = 411), with shuffling and a random state of 42. This process was used to examine the variation in accuracy and F1-score across the five folds independently of the held-out test results. The results from both evaluation methods are presented in Section V.G. Explainable AI and SHAP     MindSafe uses SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) to explain how individual features contribute to each model prediction. The TreeExplainer is applied to the Random Forest model to calculate the SHAP values for the predicted class of each respondent. For each respondent, the 54 feature contributions are ranked according to their absolute SHAP values, and the top five features are reported as the main factors influencing the prediction. Positive SHAP values indicate an increased contribution to the prediction, while negative values indicate a decreased contribution. The feature names are converted into clear, readable labels for interpretation. SHAP values describe the model's behaviour and do not indicate medical causation. In addition, the top 15 features are identified using their mean absolute SHAP values across the evaluation dataset.H. Statistical Analysis     MindSafe includes a dedicated statistical analysis module, statisticsService.js, implemented in JavaScript to examine patterns and relationships in the survey data. It provides four main types of analysis. Descriptive statistics summarize numerical variables using mean, median, mode, standard deviation, minimum, and maximum, while categorical variables are summarized using frequencies, percentages, and mode. The Chi-Square test of independence examines associations between categorical variables using contingency tables, degrees of freedom, the χ² statistic, and p-values. The independent two-sample t-test compares mean mental-health-impact scores between two groups, such as respondents who experienced cyberbullying and those who did not. Spearman rank correlation measures the strength and direction of monotonic relationships between ordinal variables.     Researchers can apply these analyses through the Bivariate Relationship Explorer and Statistical Analysis Suite. Each test is applied only when the selected variables meet the requirements of that test; for example, the Chi-Square test is used for categorical variables, while the t-test requires a two-group comparison.V. RESULTS AND DISCUSSIONA. Dataset CharacteristicsThe analytical dataset contains 514 respondents, distributed across four Mental Health Impact classes, as shown in Fig. 2. The distribution is strongly skewed toward the Not at all class, with 44.55% of respondents reporting no perceived mental-health impact, compared with only 7.98% reporting a Severe impact. This class imbalance presents a challenge for the classification task described in Section IV-E.Fig. 2. Distribution of respondents across Mental Health Impact classes (N = 514).B. Statistical Analysis ResultsThe statistical analysis included descriptive statistics, Chi-Square testing, Spearman correlation, and Welch's two-sample t-test. Descriptive results showed a mean Emotional Impact Severity of 2.100 and mean Cyberbullying Encounter Frequency of 0.951, while average daily social-media usage was 3.784 hours. Chi-Square analysis showed significant associations between Mental Health Impact and direct cyberbullying experience (χ² = 146.281, p < 0.001), encounter frequency (χ² = 51.744, p < 0.001), and incident platform (χ² = 21.736, p = 0.0406). Gender and daily social-media usage were not significantly associated with Mental Health Impact. Spearman correlation showed positive relationships between Mental Health Impact and Emotional Impact Severity (ρ = 0.319, p < 0.001) and between Mental Health Impact and Cyberbullying Encounter Frequency (ρ = 0.242, p < 0.001). Daily social-media usage showed no significant relationship with Mental Health Impact. Welch's t-test found a significant difference in Emotional Impact Severity between respondents who experienced cyberbullying and those who did not (t = 12.976, p < 0.001, Cohen's d = 1.385). The Mann–Whitney U test also confirmed this difference (p < 0.001).C. Model Comparison/Fig. 3. CONCLUSION     This paper presented MindSafe, an academic research platform that predicts a survey-defined Mental Health Impact category from cyberbullying-related survey responses. The platform uses a leakage-controlled Random Forest classifier and Tree SHAP to explain individual predictions. On a held-out test set of 103 respondents, the model achieved 48.54% accuracy and a macro F1-score of 0.3275. Its cross-validated accuracy of 49.88% ± 1.74% provides additional evidence of consistent performance across different training folds. MindSafe combines this prediction workflow with a three-tier web application using a SvelteKit frontend, FastAPI machine-learning service, and MongoDB Atlas, along with statistical tools for exploring the survey data.     The results also show the limitations of the current model. The Severely class had a recall of only 12.50%, reflecting the difficulty of predicting minority classes in the relatively small and imbalanced survey dataset (N = 514). Therefore, MindSafe is presented as a non-diagnostic research and screening-awareness platform, not as a clinical diagnostic system. It predicts a survey-defined mental health impact category rather than diagnosing a mental health condition. Future work can use larger and more diverse datasets to further evaluate and improve the approach.VII. FUTURE ENHANCEMENTS     Several areas can be improved in future work. First, the dataset should be expanded beyond 514 respondents and collected from more than one institution. This could improve the results, especially for the Severely class, which currently has a low recall of 12.50%. Second, the difference between the production and training software versions should be resolved: the production environment uses pandas 1.5.3 and SHAP 0.42.1, while training used pandas 2.2.3 and SHAP 0.49.1. Retraining and testing the model in the same environment would reduce possible compatibility problems. Third, the security limitations should be improved, since the current system uses a fallback JWT secret and has no rate limiting for the login, registration, and prediction endpoints. Fourth, collecting data from the same participants over multiple time periods could help study changes and patterns over time, although this would still not prove a clinical cause-and-effect relationship. Finally, future work could compare MindSafe's results with validated clinical mental-health questionnaires, with proper ethical approval. Until then, MindSafe should remain focused on research and educational awareness, rather than clinical use.REFERENCES[1] H. Alabdulwahab, M. A. Haq, and M.
